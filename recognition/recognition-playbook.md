# Recognition Playbook

Recognition is not a nice-to-have. It is a retention mechanism.

Champions who feel seen stay. Champions who feel invisible — regardless of how much they care about security — quietly deprioritize program work when sprint pressure mounts. This playbook defines how recognition works at every level of the program, for every audience that matters.

---

## Recognition Design Principles

**Recognition must reach multiple levels to be effective.**
Champion-level recognition maintains individual motivation. Manager and director-level recognition is what makes the program organizationally sustainable. Both are required.

**Timing matters as much as type.**
Recognition given weeks after the contribution has less impact than recognition given in the moment or the same week. Build recognition into regular rhythms so it happens consistently, not only when someone remembers.

**Recognition should be specific, not generic.**
"Great job this quarter" lands flat. "Sarah caught a critical injection vulnerability at PR stage that would have reached production" lands. Always name the contribution, not just the person.

**Pull-based recognition is the goal.**
When managers and directors are proactively requesting opportunities to recognize their champions — rather than being reminded to do so — the program has achieved organizational pull. That is the signal the program has matured.

## Recognition by Phase

### Phase 1: Relaunch and Reestablish (Months 0–6)

The goal in Phase 1 is visibility and early momentum. Recognition at this stage is frequent, informal, and public.

| Recognition Type | Cadence | Owner | Audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Informal shout-outs in champion channel | Weekly | Security team | Champions |
| Team channel acknowledgment of first contributions | As they happen | Security team + Manager | Champion's team |
| All-hands program announcement | Once, at launch | Executive sponsor | Engineering org |
| First Champion Spotlight newsletter | End of quarter | Security team | Engineering org |
| 90-day probation completion acknowledgment | At review | Security team + Manager | Champion's team and manager chain |

**What good looks like in Phase 1:**
A new champion's first successful triage is acknowledged publicly within the week it happens. Their manager hears about it from the Security team, not just from the champion. The 90-day review is a celebration, not just an evaluation.

---

### Phase 2: Land and Expand (Months 6–12)

Recognition in Phase 2 becomes more structured, more merit-based, and begins reaching director and VP level.

| Recognition Type | Cadence | Owner | Audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quarterly Champion Spotlight newsletter | Quarterly | Security team | Engineering org |
| Metrics awards (highest vuln reduction, best PR reviewer) | Quarterly | Security team | Champions + managers |
| Belt promotion certificates and badges | At promotion | Security team | Champion, manager, team |
| Manager kudos distributed to director level | Monthly | Security team provides templates; managers send | Directors and VPs |
| CTF competition recognition | Bi-annual event | Security team | Champions + engineering org |

**Manager kudos templates:**
Managers should not have to write recognition from scratch. Provide them with ready-to-send templates that they can personalize. Templates should be written for the manager's audience — their director or skip-level — and frame the champion's contribution in delivery terms, not security jargon.

Example template:
> "[Champion name] has been contributing as a Security Champion for [X months]. This quarter, they triaged [N] security findings in our pipeline, reducing our team's security review backlog by [X%]. Their work has meant fewer last-minute security blockers before release. I wanted to make sure this contribution was visible at the team and org level."

---

### Phase 3: Delegate and Accelerate (Months 12–18)

Recognition in Phase 3 reaches the engineering all-hands level and begins connecting to formal career conversations.

| Recognition Type | Cadence | Owner | Audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Champions showcase at engineering all-hands | Quarterly | Executive sponsor + Security team | Engineering org |
| Conference sponsorship for top performers | As available | Security team + budget owner | Top 2–3 champions |
| Senior Champion title documented in career framework | At Brown belt promotion | Security team + HR | Champion, manager, HR |
| Pod lead acknowledgment in organizational comms | At pod formation | Security team | Engineering org |
| Director and VP recognition in QBRs | Quarterly | Security team provides data; sponsor presents | Leadership team |

---

### Phases 4–6: Institutionalize and Sustain (Months 18–36+)

Recognition at maturity is executive-level, career-integrated, and increasingly peer-driven.

| Recognition Type | Cadence | Owner | Audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual Champions Summit and awards | Annually | Security team + executive sponsor | All champions, engineering leadership |
| CTO and CISO briefings on champion program impact | Quarterly | Program owner | Executive team |
| Multi-year service awards (2+ years) | At anniversary | Security team | Champion, manager, engineering org |
| Black Belt recognition in career framework | At promotion | Security team + HR + manager | Champion, manager, HR, leadership |
| Champion-led sessions at engineering all-hands | Ongoing | Champions (facilitated by Security team) | Engineering org |

## Recognition by Audience

Different audiences need different types of recognition. Sending the same message to everyone dilutes the signal.

### For Champions

What matters: being seen by peers, having contributions named specifically, visibility in the channels they care about.

- Public shout-outs in champion and team channels at the moment of contribution
- Belt promotion acknowledgment with a specific description of what earned it
- Spotlight features that tell their story — not just their metrics
- CTF participation and competition recognition
- Conference sponsorship for top performers

### For Managers

What matters: evidence they can use in performance conversations, visibility to their own leadership, confirmation their team investment is paying off.

- Weekly or bi-weekly summary from the Security team on their champion's contributions
- Ready-to-send kudos templates that require minimal effort to personalize and send
- Quarterly metrics showing their team's security posture improvement vs. baseline
- Explicit acknowledgment from the Security team in director forums when a manager's support has been exceptional

### For Directors and VPs

What matters: business outcomes, delivery impact, organizational capability signals.

- Quarterly program health summary in delivery terms: findings prevented at PR stage, MTTR improvement, reduced late-cycle escalations
- Champion program mentioned in QBR reporting — normalize it as a delivery metric, not a security metric
- Named recognition when a director's organization has produced strong champions or shown exceptional manager support
- Executive sponsor briefing that ties program outcomes to business goals

## Recognition Budget Guidance

Recognition does not require a large budget, but it does require an allocated one. Programs that run on goodwill and no resources signal that the organization does not actually value the contribution.

| Item | Estimated Cost | Phase |
|---|---|---|
| Digital badges and certificates | Low (design cost only) | Phase 1+ |
| CTF prizes and competition awards | Medium | Phase 2+ |
| Quarterly spotlight production | Low | Phase 1+ |
| Conference sponsorship (1–2 champions) | Medium–High | Phase 3+ |
| Annual summit and awards | Medium | Phase 4+ |
| Multi-year service awards | Low–Medium | Phase 5+ |

> **Budget tip:** Frame recognition spend to finance teams and executive sponsors as retention cost, not a program perk. Replacing an engineer costs significantly more than a conference ticket or a quarterly award. Champions who feel recognized stay. Champions who feel invisible leave — and they take the institutional security knowledge they built with them.

---

## What to Avoid

**Avoid recognition theater.**
Generic quarterly emails that list everyone's name without specific contributions feel perfunctory and can actually reduce motivation by signaling that contributions are interchangeable.

**Avoid recognition that only flows downward.**
If recognition only comes from the Security team to champions, without manager and director visibility, the program will feel like a side project rather than an organizational priority.

**Avoid tying recognition exclusively to volume metrics.**
Recognizing only the champion who triaged the most findings incentivizes quantity over quality and discourages champions working in lower-volume but higher-complexity areas. Recognize impact, not just activity.

**Avoid letting promotions go unannounced.**
Belt promotions are earned, not administrative. Every promotion should be announced publicly, named specifically, and connected to what the champion did to earn it.

**Avoid saving recognition for the annual review.**
By the time the annual review comes around, the contribution that warranted recognition has been forgotten — by the champion, their manager, and everyone else. Recognition closest to the contribution has the highest impact.
