# Why This Exists

This framework was not built in a vacuum. It was built after observing — and in some cases experiencing — the specific ways Security Champions programs fail. Understanding those failure modes is the only way to understand the design decisions that followed.

---

## The Three Failure Modes

### Failure Mode 1: The Recruiting Pipeline

Security teams identify talented engineers through the Champions program and recruit them into the security org. It feels efficient. It probably is, for a while.

Then managers figure out what is happening. They stop nominating their best people. They nominate engineers who are available, not engineers who are capable. The quality of the champion cohort degrades. Participation becomes performative. The program fades.

**The design response:** Manager buy-in is a hard requirement, not a courtesy. Champions are nominated *with* explicit manager approval and written commitment. The charter explicitly prohibits using the program as a recruiting pipeline without a separate, transparent process. Managers are co-owners, not talent suppliers.

---

### Failure Mode 2: Training Without Delegation

Champions complete modules. They earn badges. They attend sessions. They learn things that are genuinely useful.

Then they go back to their teams and nothing changes. There is no security work waiting for them to apply their new knowledge. Sprint pressure fills the space. Within a quarter, champion work is the first thing that gets deprioritized. Within two quarters, most of the cohort has quietly stopped participating.

The program was designed as a training program. It felt like a training program. It was treated like a training program. Training programs do not create behavior change — repeated application does.

**The design response:** Real, meaningful security work is delegated to champions from the first week of Yellow belt. Training is always paired with an immediate task. The belt progression is built around demonstrated contribution, not module completion. Triage, code review participation, SOP creation, and threat model involvement are the program. Training is the prerequisite for doing those things well.

---

### Failure Mode 3: No Manager Accountability

The program launches with enthusiasm. Champions are excited. The security team is optimistic. Managers nod along in the kickoff meeting.

Then sprint planning happens. The backlog is full. The deadline is real. Champion meetup is on Thursday. Guess what gets cut.

It happens once. Then again. Then it is just how things work. Champions stop expecting their time to be protected. They stop trying. Attrition follows.

The program treated manager participation as optional and goodwill-based. It was neither. Managers without accountability produce managers without commitment.

**The design response:** Manager commitment is documented and signed before a single champion is onboarded. The commitment includes specific obligations: time protection, feedback at defined intervals, active recognition, and an explicit prohibition on using the program as a recruiting tool. Manager feedback is a required gate at 30, 60, and 90 days — not a nice-to-have. When manager resistance emerges, it is escalated before it becomes attrition, not after.

## What Makes This Framework Different

Most publicly available Security Champions resources are either conceptual (here is why you should do this) or tactical (here is a training curriculum). Very few address the organizational dynamics that determine whether the program survives contact with the real engineering organization.

This framework is built around those dynamics:

**Stakeholder value is defined before recruitment begins.** The value proposition document defines what is in it for every person in the organization — from mid-level engineer to VP — in their own terms. A program that cannot answer "what's in it for me?" at every level will not sustain voluntary participation.

**The belt system connects participation to career growth.** Champions who feel like they are doing extra work for no visible benefit leave. Champions who can point to their belt level in a performance review, who have a concrete path from Yellow to Black, who see peers getting recognized and promoted, stay. The belt system is a retention mechanism disguised as a progression framework.

**Metrics are time-sequenced deliberately.** Measuring vulnerability counts in the first quarter of a relaunch is how programs get cancelled before they have a chance to work. The KPI framework measures trust and engagement first, capability transfer second, and security impact third. This reflects how organizational behavior change actually works — not how security teams wish it worked.

**Course correction is planned, not reactive.** Every phase of the roadmap includes documented levers for common problems. Programs that are surprised by attrition, manager resistance, or scope creep fail. Programs that have a pre-planned response to those signals adjust and continue.

**The program is designed to make itself unnecessary at the team level.** The 36-month target is not a Security Champions program that the security team manages indefinitely. It is an engineering organization where security is part of how teams work — where champions are recognized in career frameworks, where triage is handled without central team intervention, where the security team focuses on strategic and complex work rather than routine operations.

---

## A Note on Reuse

This framework is generalized from real program design work. It reflects real lessons from programs that failed and informed a new approach that took those failures seriously.

You will need to adapt it. Your organization's culture, engineering maturity, security team capacity, and executive appetite for this kind of program will all affect how the framework applies. The course correction levers exist because no two organizations are the same.

Use what fits. Adjust what does not. Contribute back what you learn.

---

## What This Framework Does Not Solve

Being honest about limitations is part of designing programs that last.

**It does not solve executive disinterest.** If your CISO or VP of Engineering does not believe security needs to be embedded in product teams, this framework will not change that belief. Executive sponsorship is a prerequisite, not an output.

**It does not replace security headcount.** Champions augment a security team. They do not replace one. A security team too small to support a champions program will see the program become a liability rather than a force multiplier.

**It does not work in organizations where security and engineering are adversarial.** If the relationship between security and product teams is characterized by distrust, compliance culture, or mutual contempt, a Champions program will inherit that dynamic. Relationship repair has to come first.

**It does not produce results in 90 days.** The first quarter is explicitly designed to measure trust and engagement, not security outcomes. Organizations that need to show ROI within a quarter should be honest that a 36-month culture change program is not the right tool for that goal.
